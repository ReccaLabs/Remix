Starting an online class is less about technology and more about habits. You need a room students can join, a way to see who has paid, and a place to keep your recordings. This guide walks through each part in the order you will need it.

It is written for a teacher starting alone, at home, with a laptop or a phone. If you already teach in a hall, most of it still applies.

## Decide what you are offering

Before you buy anything, write down the class in one or two lines. Students and parents will ask these questions first, so have the answers ready.

- **Subject and level.** For example, A/L Physics theory, O/L Maths revision or Grade 5 scholarship papers.
- **Batch and year.** Keep each exam year as its own batch. It makes fees and recordings much easier later.
- **Day and time.** Pick a fixed slot and keep it. A class that moves every week loses students.
- **Monthly fee and due date.** Decide the fee, the day it is due, and how many days of grace you give.
- **What a student gets.** Live class, recordings, notes (tutes), paper discussions or all of these.

Start with one batch. You can add more once the first one runs smoothly.

## The equipment you actually need

You do not need a studio. You need to be heard clearly, seen clearly, and able to keep going when the power or internet drops.

### Something to teach from

A laptop is easiest, because you can share your screen and watch the chat at the same time. A phone works for the first few weeks if that is what you have. Keep it on a stand, not in your hand.

### A way to write

Students need to see your working. Pick one of these:

- A pen tablet or a touchscreen with a writing app, shared to the class.
- A phone on a stand above a sheet of paper, joined to the meeting as a second device.
- A whiteboard behind you, with good light and a thick dark marker.

Try your choice with a friend on a phone screen. If they cannot read it on a small screen, your students cannot either.

### Sound

Sound matters more than video. A simple wired headset or a clip-on microphone is a big step up from the laptop's built-in microphone. It cuts out fan noise, traffic and echo from the room.

### Light

Sit facing a window or a lamp, not with a window behind you. A cheap desk lamp pointed at the wall in front of you gives soft, even light.

### Backup for power and internet

Plan for the day the power goes or the line stops working in the middle of a lesson.

- Keep your laptop charged before every class.
- Have mobile data on a second network ready as a hotspot.
- If power cuts are common where you live, a small UPS for your router keeps the connection alive for a while.
- Tell students in advance what happens if you drop out: wait ten minutes, and the recording will be shared if the class cannot continue.

## Set up your Zoom room properly

Most leaks and disruptions come from default settings. Spend twenty minutes on these before your first class. The exact names can change between Zoom versions, so look for the closest match in your account settings.

1. **Use a separate account for teaching.** Keep your class away from your personal meetings.
2. **Create one recurring meeting per batch.** Students always use the same room, and you always know which batch is in front of you.
3. **Turn on the waiting room.** You decide who comes in, instead of anyone with the link.
4. **Turn off "allow participants to rename themselves".** Otherwise anyone can change their name to match a paid student.
5. **Mute participants on entry,** and allow screen sharing for the host only.
6. **Turn on cloud or local recording** and test that it works.

Run a full test with a friend or family member on a phone. Check that they can hear you, read your writing and see your shared screen.

> Ask every student to join with the same name format, such as their index number and full name. It makes the waiting room and your attendance much faster to check.

For more on keeping uninvited people out, read [Stop Zoom link sharing for good](/guides/stop-zoom-link-sharing).

## Getting your first students in

Your first students usually come from people who already know you: past students, their brothers and sisters, and parents who talk to each other. Make it easy for them to share one clear message.

- A short description of the class, the day and time, and the fee.
- How to register: a phone number or a simple form, not the Zoom link.
- When the first class is, and whether it is free to try.

Keep the Zoom link out of public groups and social media posts. Send it privately to each registered student instead. It takes a little longer now, but it saves you from strangers in your class later.

Use one announcement group per batch for timetable changes and reminders. Set it so only you can post, so important messages do not get lost.

## Collecting fees from day one

Fees are where many new classes get tired and give up. Decide the rules before the first class, write them down, and apply them the same way for everyone.

- **One due date for everyone,** for example the 5th of each month, with a short grace period.
- **One way to pay online,** such as a bank transfer to an account in your name, with the slip sent to you. Cash can be paid in person if you also teach in a hall.
- **Ask for the student's name and index number** in the transfer reference, so you can match slips quickly.
- **Keep one register.** A simple spreadsheet with a row per student and a column per month is enough to start.
- **Send one polite reminder** a few days before the due date, and one after the grace period.

If a student has not paid after the grace period, remove access to the next month's recordings rather than arguing in the group. It is fairer and calmer for everyone. For a full routine, read [Collecting fees without chasing students on WhatsApp](/guides/collect-fees-without-chasing).

## Keeping your recordings safe

Recordings are valuable to your students, which also makes them the thing most likely to be shared. You cannot make leaks impossible, but you can make them rare and easy to trace.

- **Share recordings only with students who have paid for that month.**
- **Do not post recording links in open groups.** Send them to paid students, or put them somewhere that asks students to sign in.
- **Remove access when a student leaves** or stops paying.
- **Add a visible mark** with your name or class name, so a leaked copy still points back to you.
- **Keep your own copy** on a drive you control, organised by batch and month.

If a lesson does leak, stay calm. Remind the class of the rules, check who had access that month, and speak to that student and their parents privately.

## From ten students to a hundred

At ten students, you can do everything by hand. You know every face, you can check each slip yourself, and a spreadsheet is fine.

At around fifty, things start to slip. Matching bank slips takes an evening. Students ask for recordings they have not paid for. Someone joins with a borrowed name. This is the point to make your routine strict: fixed due dates, one payment method, one place for recordings, and a helper for the busy days.

Near a hundred, you are running a small institute. You need records you can trust, reminders that go out without you, and a way to give each student access only to what they paid for. ## Your first month checklist

- Write the class description, fee and due date.
- Set up a separate teaching account and one recurring meeting per batch.
- Turn on the waiting room and turn off renaming.
- Test sound, writing and recording with a friend on a phone.
- Prepare your backup: charged laptop and a mobile hotspot.
- Create a register with a row per student and a column per month.
- Send the Zoom link privately to each registered student.
- After the first class, share the recording only with paid students.

Start small, keep the rules simple, and apply them the same way every month. Students notice when a class is well organised, and they tell their friends.
